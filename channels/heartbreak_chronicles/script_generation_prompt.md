Write a 1,050-1,350 word first-person narrated story about [TOPIC]. This is a dramatic relationship betrayal story told from the perspective of the betrayed partner (husband or wife).

RETENTION STRUCTURE — every section must make the viewer feel they CANNOT stop listening:

1. COLD OPEN — THE MOMENT (first 3-4 sentences, ~30 seconds):
Drop the listener into the most intense moment of the story — mid-action, mid-emotion. NOT the beginning. Example: "I was standing in our kitchen holding her phone, and what I saw on that screen made my hands shake so badly I almost dropped it." Then immediately plant an OPEN LOOP: "But I'm getting ahead of myself. To understand why that night destroyed everything, I need to take you back to where it started." This teases the climax so the viewer MUST keep listening to get there.

2. THE SETUP — PERFECT ON THE SURFACE (~150-200 words):
Briefly paint the picture of what the relationship looked like from the outside — happy, stable, maybe even envied by friends. Use one or two hyper-specific details that make it feel real (a favorite restaurant, a nickname, a shared routine). Then drop the FIRST CRACK: one small detail that, looking back, was the first sign something was off. End this section with a forward hook: "That should have been my first warning. But what came next was so much worse."

3. THE UNRAVELING — EVIDENCE STACKS UP (~250-350 words):
The narrator discovers clues one by one. Each discovery should be its own mini-story with setup and payoff. Start with something small and ambiguous (could be innocent), then escalate to undeniable. Use the "just when you think you know the full picture" technique — reveal a new layer that makes it worse than the listener expected. Key retention moves:
- After the first piece of evidence: "If that was all I found, maybe I could have let it go. But then I checked..."
- After a bigger revelation: "And that's when I realized this wasn't just a one-time thing."
- Plant what's coming: "I knew exactly what I was going to do. But I had to be patient."
Each clue should trigger a stronger emotional reaction in the narrator — confusion → suspicion → sick feeling → cold rage.

4. THE PLAN — CALCULATED, NOT IMPULSIVE (~100-150 words):
The narrator decides to confront — but not in a hot-headed way. They set a trap or orchestrate a reveal. This should feel satisfying and clever. Tease the confrontation without revealing what happens: "I spent the next three days acting completely normal. She had no idea what was coming."

5. THE CONFRONTATION — THE PAYOFF (~300-400 words):
This is what they've been waiting for. Place this in the back 40% of the script. The narrator reveals what they know. Write this scene in SLOW MOTION — stretch every second. Describe:
- The exact setting (where, when, who's present)
- The narrator's calm delivery vs the cheater's crumbling composure
- Physical reactions in extreme detail: color draining from face, mouth opening with no words coming out, hands gripping the table edge, eyes darting to the door
- If the cheater tries to lie or deny, show the narrator calmly dismantling each excuse with evidence
- The moment the cheater realizes there's no way out — this is the emotional climax
- If there's a third party or witnesses, describe their reactions too

6. THE AFTERMATH — STRENGTH, NOT SADNESS (~50-80 words):
Quick, punchy resolution. What happened next — divorce, moving out, starting fresh. End on the narrator's strength and peace, NOT bitterness. The last line should feel like closure with an edge. Something the listener will remember.

PACING & RETENTION TECHNIQUES:
- Use OPEN LOOPS constantly — tease something coming before you deliver it. "What I found in that drawer would change everything." Then don't reveal it for another 2-3 sentences.
- RE-HOOK every 60-90 seconds of narration (~150-225 words). Drop a line that makes the listener think "wait, WHAT?" Examples: "But that wasn't even the worst part." / "And then I saw who the other person was." / "What she said next made my blood run cold."
- VARY THE PACING — short, punchy sentences during high-tension moments. Longer, flowing sentences during emotional reflection. Never let the rhythm become predictable.
- SENSORY DETAILS — don't just describe what happened, describe what it felt like physically. The pit in the stomach, the ringing in the ears, the way the room seemed to tilt.
- NEVER MORALIZE — no lessons, no "if you're going through this..." — just the raw story. The listener draws their own conclusions.
- CONVERSATIONAL TONE — write like someone telling their close friend what happened. Natural speech patterns, not literary prose. Contractions, interruptions ("And I — look, I'm not proud of what I did next"), rhetorical questions ("You know that feeling when your whole world just... stops?").

NAMES & DETAILS:
- Give the cheating partner a name (varied — not always the same names)
- Give the affair partner a name when revealed
- Use specific locations (a particular restaurant, neighborhood, workplace)
- Use specific times ("It was a Tuesday, around 11 PM")
- These details make it feel like a real story being recounted
