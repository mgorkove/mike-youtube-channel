Write a first-person narrated story about [TOPIC]. The word count will be specified below — follow the CRITICAL RULES for exact length. This is a dramatic family revenge story told from the perspective of a woman in her 20s who has been mistreated by her family and finally stands up for herself.

RETENTION STRUCTURE — every section must make the viewer feel they CANNOT stop listening:

1. COLD OPEN — THE MOMENT (first 3-4 sentences, ~30 seconds):
Drop the listener into the most intense moment of the story — mid-action, mid-emotion. NOT the beginning. Example: "I was standing in front of 200 guests at my sister's wedding when my father grabbed the microphone and called me 'the family disgrace.' What he didn't know was that the bride was alive because of me." Then immediately plant an OPEN LOOP: "But I need to take you back to where this started — sixteen years ago, when he kicked me out." This teases the climax so the viewer MUST keep listening to get there.

2. THE SETUP — THE FAMILY DYNAMIC (~1,700-1,900 words):
Paint the picture of the family structure and the narrator's role in it. Establish the power dynamics — who was the golden child, who was the scapegoat, who enabled the abuse. Use one or two hyper-specific details that make it feel real (a specific comment Mom always made, a tradition the narrator was excluded from, a room she wasn't allowed in). Then drop the FIRST CRACK: one specific incident that captures the pattern of mistreatment. End this section with a forward hook: "That was just the beginning. What they did next was so much worse."

3. THE ESCALATION — YEARS OF MISTREATMENT (~3,000-3,300 words):
Show the pattern of mistreatment escalating over time. Each incident should be its own mini-story with setup and payoff. Start with childhood slights and build to adult-level betrayal. Use the "just when you think you know the full picture" technique — reveal a new layer that makes it worse than the listener expected. Key retention moves:
- After the first incident: "If that was the worst of it, maybe I could have let it go. But then came..."
- After a bigger betrayal: "And that's when I realized this wasn't just favoritism. They actually didn't see me as family."
- Plant what's coming: "Something inside me shifted that day. I didn't know it yet, but I was done."
Each incident should trigger a stronger emotional reaction — hurt → confusion → anger → cold resolve.

4. THE BREAKING POINT — THE FINAL STRAW (~900-1,100 words):
One specific event that pushes the narrator past her limit. This should feel different from the previous incidents — bigger, more public, or more personally devastating. It triggers the narrator's decision to act. Tease the response without revealing it: "That night I sat in my car for two hours. When I finally drove home, I knew exactly what I was going to do."

5. THE REVENGE — THE PAYOFF (~3,100-3,400 words):
This is what they've been waiting for. Place this in the back 40% of the script. The narrator executes her plan — smart, calculated, and devastating. Write this scene in SLOW MOTION — stretch every second. Describe:
- The exact setting (where, when, who's present)
- The narrator's calm composure vs the family's crumbling facade
- Physical reactions in extreme detail: color draining from faces, mouths opening with no words, hands gripping the table edge, eyes darting around the room
- If family members try to deflect or gaslight, show the narrator calmly dismantling each excuse with evidence or truth
- The moment the family realizes there's no way out — this is the emotional climax
- If there are witnesses (other relatives, in-laws, guests), describe their reactions — gasps, silence, someone slowly nodding

6. THE AFTERMATH — STRENGTH, NOT BITTERNESS (~800-900 words):
Quick, punchy resolution. What happened after — going no-contact, thriving, building her own life. End on the narrator's peace and strength, NOT revenge satisfaction. The last line should feel like hard-won wisdom. Something the listener will carry with them.

PACING & RETENTION TECHNIQUES:
- Use OPEN LOOPS constantly — tease something coming before you deliver it. "What I found in that drawer would change everything." Then don't reveal it for another 2-3 sentences.
- RE-HOOK every 60-90 seconds of narration (~150-225 words). Drop a line that makes the listener think "wait, WHAT?" Examples: "But that wasn't even the worst part." / "And then I found out who told them." / "What she said next made my blood run cold."
- VARY THE PACING — short, punchy sentences during high-tension moments. Longer, flowing sentences during emotional reflection. Never let the rhythm become predictable.
- SENSORY DETAILS — don't just describe what happened, describe what it felt like physically. The pit in the stomach, the ringing in the ears, the way the room seemed to tilt.
- NEVER MORALIZE — no lessons, no "if you're going through this..." — just the raw story. The listener draws their own conclusions.
- CONVERSATIONAL TONE — write like a young woman telling her close friend what happened. Natural speech patterns, not literary prose. Contractions, interruptions ("And I — look, I'm not proud of what I did next"), rhetorical questions ("You know that feeling when your whole world just... shifts?").

NAMES & DETAILS:
- Give family members names (varied — not always the same names)
- Give specific locations (a particular restaurant, neighborhood, childhood home)
- Use specific times ("It was the Sunday before Thanksgiving, around 4 PM")
- Reference specific ages and time spans ("I was 14 when it started", "For eight years, I kept my mouth shut")
- These details make it feel like a real story being recounted

CHARACTER VOICE:
- The narrator is a woman in her 20s — smart, self-aware, emotionally mature beyond her years because of what she's been through
- She's not whiny or self-pitying — she's matter-of-fact about what happened to her
- She has a dry sense of humor about the absurdity of her family
- When she describes her revenge, there's a quiet satisfaction — not gloating
- She's moved past anger into clarity
