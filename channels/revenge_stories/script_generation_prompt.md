Write a 1,400-1,700 word first-person narrated family revenge story about [TOPIC]. This is a dramatic, satisfying story told from the perspective of an older man — a father, husband, grandfather, or father-in-law — who has been disrespected, betrayed, or taken advantage of by his own family, and delivers devastating but calculated justice.

THE NARRATOR:
- Always an older man (50s-70s). He's worked hard his whole life, built something, provided for his family
- He's calm, patient, and methodical — NOT hot-headed. He plans his revenge carefully
- He has quiet dignity. When he finally acts, it's devastating BECAUSE he was so patient
- He's relatable to the audience: a regular guy who's been pushed too far by people who should have respected him

THE ANTAGONISTS (vary across videos):
- Ungrateful adult son who takes dad's money/house/business for granted and disrespects him
- Entitled adult daughter who sides with her spouse against her father
- Manipulative wife or ex-wife trying to take everything in a divorce or turn the kids against him
- Arrogant son-in-law who disrespects the father-in-law, thinks he's better than him
- Entitled daughter-in-law who poisons the son against his father
- Greedy siblings or relatives scheming over inheritance or the family business
- Adult children who dump dad in a nursing home and fight over his assets while he's still alive
- Grandchildren who disrespect grandpa, encouraged by their entitled parents

RETENTION STRUCTURE — every section must make the viewer feel they CANNOT stop listening:

1. COLD OPEN — THE MOMENT (first 3-4 sentences, ~30 seconds):
Drop the listener into the most satisfying moment — the family member realizing they've made a terrible mistake. NOT the beginning. Example: "The look on my son's face when the lawyer read out the will — I wish I could frame it. Twenty years of taking me for granted, and he never once considered that I might have a plan of my own." Then plant an OPEN LOOP: "But to understand why he had no one to blame but himself, I need to take you back to the beginning." This teases the payoff so the viewer MUST keep listening.

2. THE SETUP — WHAT I BUILT (~200-250 words):
Paint the picture of what the narrator built — his career, business, home, family. Show how much he sacrificed and provided. Use hyper-specific details that make it feel real (working double shifts, building the house with his own hands, putting the kids through college, a specific family tradition). Then show the FIRST CRACK — the first sign of disrespect. The kid who stopped calling. The wife who started making decisions without him. The son-in-law who made a snide comment at Thanksgiving. End with a forward hook: "That should have been my wake-up call. But I kept telling myself it was just a phase."

3. THE ESCALATION — DISRESPECT PILES UP (~350-450 words):
The disrespect, betrayal, or exploitation gets worse over time. Each incident should be its own mini-story. Start with something small (dismissive comments, being excluded from decisions) and escalate to outrageous (trying to take his house, demanding money, putting him in a home, turning other family members against him). Key retention moves:
- After the first incident: "If that was all, I probably would have let it go. But then came Thanksgiving."
- After a bigger betrayal: "And that's when I realized — they didn't see me as their father. They saw me as a bank account."
- Plant what's coming: "Something in me shifted that day. I didn't say a word. But I started making calls."
Each incident should trigger a stronger emotional reaction — hurt → disappointment → cold clarity.

4. THE PLAN — QUIET AND CALCULATED (~100-150 words):
The narrator decides to act — but not in anger. He consults a lawyer, rewrites the will, sets up a trust, makes quiet business moves, or arranges a family gathering where the truth will come out. This should feel methodical and inevitable. The narrator knows exactly what's going to happen. Tease the payoff: "I spent the next two months getting everything in order. They had no idea what was coming. None of them did."

5. THE RECKONING — THE PAYOFF (~400-500 words):
This is what they've been waiting for. Place this in the back 40% of the script. Write this in SLOW MOTION — stretch every moment. Describe:
- The setting: a family dinner, a lawyer's office, a holiday gathering, a business meeting
- The narrator calmly delivering the news or revealing the truth
- The family member's reaction in extreme detail: the smirk fading, the color draining, the jaw dropping, the stammering excuses
- If they try to argue or guilt-trip, show the narrator calmly dismantling their position with facts, receipts, or documents
- The moment they realize there's no undoing it — this is the emotional climax
- Other family members' reactions: the ones who stayed loyal being rewarded, the ones who betrayed him being shocked
- The concrete consequence: cut from the will, fired from the family business, evicted from the property, exposed in front of the whole family

6. THE AFTERMATH — PEACE AND DIGNITY (~80-120 words):
Quick, punchy resolution. What happened next — the narrator's life improved, the disrespectful family member faced consequences, relationships with loyal family members strengthened. End on the narrator's peace and self-respect, NOT bitterness. The last line should land with quiet satisfaction. Something like: "My son calls every Sunday now. Funny how that works."

PACING & RETENTION TECHNIQUES:
- Use OPEN LOOPS constantly — tease something coming before you deliver it. "What my daughter didn't know was that I'd already talked to the lawyer." Then don't reveal the details for 2-3 sentences.
- RE-HOOK every 60-90 seconds of narration (~150-225 words). Drop a line that makes the listener think "wait, WHAT?" Examples: "But that wasn't even the worst part." / "And then I found out where the money had really been going." / "What he said at that dinner — I'll never forget it."
- VARY THE PACING — short, punchy sentences during the payoff. Longer, reflective sentences during the setup. Never let the rhythm become predictable.
- SENSORY DETAILS — describe physical reactions. The narrator's jaw clenching, the son's smirk disappearing, the silence in the room when the truth comes out.
- NEVER MORALIZE — no lessons, no "if you're going through this..." — just the raw story. The listener draws their own conclusions.
- CONVERSATIONAL TONE — write like an older man telling his buddy what happened at the bar. Natural speech patterns, contractions, interruptions ("And I — look, I'm not a vindictive man. But enough is enough."), rhetorical questions ("You raise them, you provide for them, and this is how they repay you?").

NAMES & DETAILS:
- Give all family members names (varied — not always the same names)
- Use specific locations (a particular neighborhood, restaurant, the family property)
- Use specific times ("It was the day after Christmas, and I'd just gotten off the phone with my lawyer")
- Reference specific amounts when money is involved (not vague — "$340,000 equity", "the $50 a month he promised")
- These details make it feel like a real story being recounted
